Your first keg project can stall even with plenty of wood and metal: the missing ingredient is Oak Resin, which has its own production schedule. Tappers solve that problem before it becomes urgent. This guide focuses on a small working grove, rather than filling every spare tile with equipment you cannot yet use.

> **At a glance**
>
> - In 1.6, the basic Tapper recipe unlocks at **Foraging level 4**.
> - Each costs **40 Wood and 2 Copper Bars**.
> - Oak gives Resin, Maple gives Syrup, and Pine gives Tar.
> - Start with the product needed for your next recipe, then diversify.
> - Ordinary oak, maple, and pine tappers keep working through Winter.

## Choose the product before the tree

The three common tree outputs are not interchangeable. Oak Resin supports keg expansion; Maple Syrup is an ingredient in Bee Houses; Pine Tar has its own crafting uses. Read the recipe you intend to build before allocating your first bars. The [Tapper reference](https://wiki.stardewvalley.net/Tapper) identifies the trees and their products.

A sensible first target is one of each tree, with extra oaks if kegs are your next substantial project. This is a planning suggestion, not a universal ratio. If honey is the main attraction, your immediate syrup demand may be higher. Our [keg and jar comparison](/guides/keg-vs-preserves-jar/) and [Bee House guide](/guides/bee-house-flower-honey-guide/) explain those downstream choices.

Do not infer the tree type from where it happens to grow. Inspect the tree and its seed, and compare it with the reference if you are unsure. Repositioning a mistaken setup is possible, but waiting through the wrong production cycle is avoidable.

## Budget a grove in batches

Using the recipe above, three tappers require **120 Wood and 6 Copper Bars**. Six require **240 Wood and 12 Copper Bars**. These totals are simple multiples, and exclude everything else competing for those resources.

Before building, set aside the bars for any planned tool upgrade or essential machine. A grove is meant to remove a production delay, not create a new one at the furnace. If ore is scarce, use the [beginner mining routine](/guides/mining-guide-beginners/) to work toward a specific batch rather than wandering for unspecified supplies.

Put the first machines on trees you already have. A perfect orchard outline is not required to begin collecting. You can make a more permanent arrangement after learning how much of each product you actually consume.

## Expect different collection schedules

A basic tapper takes **seven nights for Oak Resin, nine for Maple Syrup, and five for Pine Tar**, according to the linked reference. That means a mixed grove will not all finish together simply because you placed it on the same day.

Build your routine around checking the route, not around remembering three countdowns. If you pass the grove on the way to another job, collect anything ready and store it nearby. Keep outputs in separate stacks so a shortage is visible when you open the chest.

The practical benefit of multiple oaks is a larger batch over time. Adding more does not shorten an individual tree's wait. If tomorrow's recipe needs resin and every tapper was installed today, the solution is to work on another project while the supply starts.

## Leave walking and harvesting space

A compact grove can still be inconvenient. Before committing to more trees, walk through the layout as though you are collecting from every trunk. Leave access to the machines and an obvious exit toward your daily route.

Use a designated area for woodcutting so a routine clearing session does not remove your production trees. Our [farm layout guide](/guides/farm-layout-tips/) is useful here: separate resource production from routes and livestock space before adding decorative structure.

For a very small farm, a short accessible row can be easier to maintain than a dense grid. You lose little by starting with a layout you understand, then expanding once the output becomes a regular part of your crafting.

## What to keep and what to sell

Give stored products a purpose. You might label a reserve mentally as “the next few kegs” or “the next Bee House batch.” Keep enough for that plan, then reconsider what a growing surplus is doing for you.

Selling every collection can leave your next expansion waiting. Keeping every collection forever can leave useful income idle. Review the reserve when your goals change, especially once you have finished the machinery you were saving toward.

## Troubleshooting the first grove

If the output seems wrong, check the tree type. If it seems slow, compare the correct product's schedule rather than another tree's timer. If the grove is difficult to use, fix access before doubling its size. These checks are cheaper than building replacements on the assumption that more equipment solves every delay.

Tappers cannot be used on fruit trees. For an orchard, read the [fruit tree planting guide](/guides/fruit-tree-planting-guide/) instead.
